Blackjack looks like the easiest game in the casino to walk up to, and mechanically it is.
The part nobody explains up front is the handful of physical and social habits a real table
expects that a rulebook never mentions. This covers those: what to check before you sit down,
how to talk to your cards instead of touching them, and what actually costs you money before
a single card is dealt.

## Buying in and finding the right table

Table minimums are posted on a small placard at the corner of the table, usually $10, $15,
$25, or $50 depending on the pit and the time of day, with higher minimums generally clustered
closer to the pit boss's line of sight and lower minimums further out or during slower hours.
Sit down, put cash on the layout (not in the dealer's hand), and wait for the dealer to convert
it to chips. If a table's minimum is higher than you want to bet, walk to a different one;
nobody will stop you, and pit staff would rather you find a comfortable table than sit down and
feel pressured into betting more than you meant to.

An empty seat doesn't guarantee you can join mid-shoe. If a hand is already in progress when
you walk up, wait for it to finish before sitting down and asking to buy in; joining
mid-hand is occasionally restricted by house rule, and even where it's allowed it's
considered a minor courtesy to wait.

## Reading the table placard

The same small sign that lists the minimum also lists the rules that actually matter: number
of decks, whether the dealer hits or stands on soft 17, and the blackjack payout. **Say this
directly: if the placard says blackjack pays 6:5 instead of 3:2, walk away.** On a $10 bet,
3:2 pays $15 for a natural; 6:5 pays $12. That's not a rounding difference, it's a real cut to
the one payout in the game that's supposed to reward you extra for good luck, and it raises
the house edge by roughly 1.4 percentage points on its own, more than every other rule
variation on the placard combined. A single-deck 6:5 table is not a better game because it
has fewer decks; the payout cut outweighs it easily.

Dealer soft-17 rules matter too, just less dramatically. A dealer who hits soft 17 (H17)
gives the house a slightly bigger edge than one who stands (S17), typically around 0.2
percentage points, since the dealer gets one more shot at improving a marginal hand. It's
worth checking, but it's not the same category of decision as the 6:5 payout question. Fewer
decks generally favor the player slightly too, everything else held equal, though the effect
is smaller than either of the other two.

Say "hit" out loud if you want, but back it with the tap. Say "stand," but back it with the
wave. The signal is what the dealer and the eye in the sky both trust; a verbal call with no
matching gesture can get a dealer asking you to confirm, which just slows the table down
for everyone.

## Shoe games vs. pitch games

Most tables today deal from a shoe holding 6 or 8 decks, with cards dealt face up and never
touched by players at all; you play entirely by hand signal. Older or lower-stakes "pitch"
games deal 1 or 2 decks face down directly into your hand, and there you're allowed to pick
up your cards, one hand only, and hold them above the table at all times where everyone can
still see they haven't been swapped or marked. If you're not sure which kind of game you're
at, watch whether the cards land face up or face down; that answers it immediately. This
site's [blackjack table](/games/blackjack/) deals from a 6-deck shoe face up, matching the
far more common modern format.

Either way, chips stay off your cards at all times, even in a pitch game where you're holding
them. Betting more once you've seen your hand is exactly what the game is built to prevent,
which is why the dealer will always ask you to keep chips and cards physically separate.

## Splitting and doubling mechanics

Doubling and splitting both mean placing a second bet, and it always goes down next to your
original bet, never on top of it or combined with it. The dealer needs both bets visible and
separately countable to pay or collect correctly, both for their own accuracy and because
surveillance needs to be able to reconstruct exactly what was wagered from the video alone.
Once you've doubled, you get exactly one more card and your turn ends regardless of the
total, win or bust. Once you've split, each half becomes its own hand with its own bet,
played out in order, left hand first, and the dealer will slide your cards apart to keep the
two hands visually distinct as you go.

Never touch either bet once it's placed, and never try to consolidate a split or doubled hand
back into a single stack mid-round. If you want to know how much is riding on a given hand,
ask the dealer rather than reaching in to count it yourself.

## The insurance prompt

When the dealer's up card is an Ace, you'll hear "insurance, anyone?" before anything else
happens. Insurance is a side bet that the dealer's hole card is a ten, paying 2:1, and it has
nothing to do with protecting your own hand even though the name suggests otherwise. It's
placed on a separate strip of the layout marked "INSURANCE PAYS 2 TO 1," for exactly half
your original bet, and it settles immediately, before your own hand is even played. If you're
offered it and don't want it, "no" or a small headshake is all that's needed; nobody will ask
twice. You'll also sometimes hear "even money" offered specifically when you have a natural
blackjack yourself and the dealer shows an Ace; that's the same bet wearing a different name,
just as bad an idea despite sounding like a guaranteed win. For the actual math on why
insurance is a losing bet almost every time it's offered, see [insurance,
simulated](/the-pit/blackjack-insurance-simulated/).

## Etiquette

Put your phone away once cards are in play; a screen out during a hand is one of the more
consistently enforced rules at any table, since it's treated as a potential distraction and,
at the wrong table, a potential signaling risk. Don't touch your chips once the cards are out
for the hand, win or lose; wait for the dealer to settle the hand before you move anything.

Playing a hand differently than basic strategy says, hitting a stiff hand or standing early,
doesn't cost the table anything mathematically no matter how loudly a nearby player might
complain about it. Every hand at the table is independent of every other hand at the same
table; nobody else's cards change because of how you played yours, and the shoe's next card
was already determined before anyone's turn started, not shuffled in reaction to your
decision. It's a common and understandable superstition at a full table, and also just not
how a shuffled shoe works.

## Tipping

The same two options as any table game: hand chips directly to a dealer, or make a bet for
them that they keep if it wins, usually placed just outside your own betting box and often
called a "dealer bet." A losing dealer bet doesn't cost you anything beyond what you already
set aside for it. Tipping isn't required, but a small toke now and then, especially during a
winning stretch, is the norm at any table you're staying at for more than a few hands.

## Bringing a strategy card

Small printed basic-strategy cards are allowed at nearly every blackjack table in the
country; dealers see them constantly and won't blink. Nobody will make you feel unwelcome for
checking one before you act, and it's the single fastest way to play close to the mathematically
correct line without memorizing a full chart first. Practice the chart itself on the free
[blackjack table](/games/blackjack/) until the signals above start feeling automatic.
