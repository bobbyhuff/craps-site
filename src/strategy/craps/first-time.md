Knowing the rules and knowing how to stand at a real table are two different skills. Reading
about the come-out roll doesn't tell you where to put your cash down, who to ask for a Place
bet, or why everyone just groaned at the guy who said "seven." This covers the second skill:
what to actually do, physically and socially, the first time you walk up to a live
[craps](/games/craps/) game. None of it is hard. Most of it just isn't written down anywhere
obvious, because everyone who already knows it learned by watching, not by reading.

## Buying in

Don't hand cash to a dealer directly. Wait for a break in the action (between rolls, not
mid-shooter), put your bills flat on the layout in front of an open spot, and say what you
want: "change two hundred," or just wait for the dealer to ask. The dealer converts it to
chips, stacked in the denominations you'll need to actually bet with, and slides them to you.
This is standard everywhere and dealers expect it; nobody thinks less of a new player for not
knowing the exact phrase, only for handing money directly into a dealer's hand, which
security treats as suspicious no matter who does it, since it's the one gesture that looks
like an attempt to pass something off the table's cameras.

Count your chips before you walk away from the table, and count them again if you cash out.
Mistakes happen rarely, but they're far easier to fix in the moment than after you've left
the pit.

## Where to stand and why the spot matters

Any open rail spot works, but the spot changes what's easy to bet. Standing near a base
dealer makes Place, Buy, and Come bets simple since you can just tell that dealer what you
want and they're right in front of you. Standing near the stickman's end puts you closer to
the proposition bets in the center but farther from a base dealer for the numbers, meaning
you'll be calling bets out over more distance. New players do fine anywhere; it only matters
once you're betting several things at once and don't want to reach or shout across people to
get a dealer's attention.

If the table's crowded, standing near a corner tends to be the easiest spot for a first
session: it puts you within reach of a base dealer for Place bets while keeping you out of
the more crowded center where the loudest, most complicated action happens.

Four people run the game. The **stickman** controls the stick, moves the dice back to the
shooter, calls out every roll, and runs the center proposition bets. The **boxman** sits,
watches for irregularities, and handles color changes and fills, the casino's own money
supervisor at the table. The two **base dealers** stand on either side of the boxman, each
running one half of the number layout: taking bets, paying wins, and collecting losses on
their side.

## What you can and can't touch

Chips and cash on the layout are yours to place; the stickman's stick, the dice before
they're thrown to you, and anything already in a dealer's rack are not. The one rule that
matters most: never reach across the table while a shooter is mid-roll. Dice in the air or
about to be thrown means hands off the layout until the roll settles and the dealers finish
paying, both because it's genuinely dangerous to the dealers' ability to track every bet
correctly and because a hand near the dice mid-roll is exactly what surveillance is trained
to flag.

Once the dice land and the dealers start paying out, that's the moment to place new bets or
pick up chips you're taking down, not before. If you're not sure whether it's safe to touch
the layout, wait a beat and watch what the regulars around you are doing.

## Placing bets

Pass Line, Don't Pass, and Field bets go down yourself, directly on the printed area in front
of you. Odds, Come, and Don't Come bets also go down yourself once you know where: Odds sit
just behind your Pass Line bet, Come and Don't Come go in the box marked for them. Place
bets, Buy bets, and Lay bets get called out to a base dealer instead ("Place the six for
twelve," "Buy the four for twenty-five") since they land on a number box across the layout
that you can't physically reach. Proposition bets in the center get tossed to the stickman
with the amount stated out loud, since that's the stickman's territory, not yours to reach
into; the stickman places the chip on the correct box for you.

If you're ever unsure which category a bet falls into, just say what you want out loud to
whichever dealer is closest. Every dealer at the table has heard every version of "I'm not
sure how to say this" and will either place it themselves or point you to whoever should.

## Shooting

When the dice come to you, pick them up with one hand only, not two, since a two-handed pickup
is one of the more common cues pit staff watch for as a sign of attempted manipulation. Toss
them so they hit the felt wall on the far end of the table; a roll that doesn't reach the wall
can be disallowed and re-thrown at the boxman's discretion, since a roll that never contacted
the wall is easier to influence than a properly bounced one. Look at the numbers before you
throw and try not to hold up the game for too long deciding, since a table full of people is
waiting on every roll and a slow shooter is one of the few things that reliably annoys an
entire table at once.

Shooting isn't mandatory. If the dice reach you and you'd rather not throw, just say "pass"
and they move to the next player. Nobody is required to shoot to play craps at all; betting
on someone else's roll is the default for most of the table, most of the time.

## Superstitions you'll get scolded for

Don't say "seven" out loud once a point is on. It's pure superstition with zero effect on the
actual dice, but it's the one norm enforced hardest at a live table, and someone will correct
you fast if you break it, sometimes sharply, since a whole table's worth of Pass Line and Odds
money is riding on that number not showing up. If you need to reference it, "the number
nobody says" or just gesturing works fine.

Setting the dice to a specific face before throwing is tolerated at a relaxed table and
rushed past at a busy one; the physics of a fair, properly bounced roll overwhelms any
pre-throw arrangement, so it doesn't change anything about the outcome either way. Blowing on
the dice, touching them to a lucky item, or any of the dozen other rituals regulars have are
all in the same category: harmless to the game, meaningful to the person doing them. Neither
category of habit affects the math. Both affect how fast you're accepted at the table, and a
new player who respects the "seven" rule specifically will be treated like a regular faster
than one who doesn't.

## Tipping

Tipping isn't required but it's normal and expected at any table you're staying at for a
while, especially if you're winning. Two common ways: hand chips directly to a dealer ("this
is for you guys"), which they split among the crew, or make a bet on the dealers' behalf that
they keep if it wins, usually a Place bet or a bet riding alongside your own ("and a five for
the guys" on top of your own Pass Line bet). A bet for the dealers costs nothing extra if it
loses; it was already earmarked for them either way, and dealers generally appreciate a toke
bet slightly more than a flat handout since it gives them a stake in the same outcome you're
hoping for.

There's no fixed amount. A toke here and there over a session, especially after a win, reads
as generous without needing to be large.

## When bets are working and when they're off

Place, Buy, and Hardway bets default to off during a come-out roll at most real tables,
meaning they can't win or lose until a point is established, unless you tell the dealer
you want them working. Practice this on the free [craps table](/games/craps/) before you
play live: the sim table defaults Place and Buy bets to working from the moment you make
them, including through the come-out, which is more permissive than most real tables. Toggle
its own Bets Off control if you want to match the real-table default while you practice.

## You will not look stupid if

You buy in wrong, bet on the wrong spot, or ask a dealer to repeat something. Every regular
at every table did all of that once. The only things that draw real irritation are reaching
across a live roll and saying "seven" at the wrong moment, and now you know both. Everything
else is just a new player learning, which every dealer has seen a thousand times and doesn't
think twice about.
